# Probate Process Guide

## What Is Probate?

Probate is the court-supervised legal process for transferring a deceased person's assets to their heirs or beneficiaries. When someone dies owning real property solely in their name, that property typically cannot be sold until the probate court authorizes the transfer. This creates a window of motivated selling opportunity for investors.

## How the Process Works

**Filing:** After a death, an interested party (usually a family member) files a petition with the probate court in the county where the decedent lived. The court opens a case and appoints a personal representative (PR), also called an executor if named in the will or administrator if appointed by the court.

**Inventory:** The PR is required to inventory all estate assets, including real property. This inventory becomes part of the public court record — which is one of the primary data sources for probate investors.

**Notice period:** Most states require a creditor notice period of 90 to 120 days during which creditors can file claims against the estate. Property typically cannot be sold during this window without court approval.

**Court authorization:** Once the notice period passes and creditor claims are resolved, the PR may sell real property. In some states (formal probate), each sale requires a court confirmation hearing. In others (independent administration), the PR can sell without court approval.

**Distribution and closing:** After property is sold and all claims paid, remaining assets are distributed to heirs and the estate is closed.

## Typical Timeline

| Phase | Duration |
|-------|----------|
| Filing to PR appointment | 2-6 weeks |
| Creditor notice period | 90-120 days |
| Listing/negotiation window | 30-90 days |
| Court confirmation (if required) | 30-45 days |
| Total from death to close | 6-18 months |

The sweet spot for investor outreach is months 3-9 after filing — the family has processed their grief, the notice period is ending or complete, and the PR is now motivated to resolve the estate efficiently.

## When Properties Become Available

Properties enter the market through probate in three ways:

1. **Direct sale to investor:** PR accepts a cash offer without listing on MLS, often to close quickly and avoid agent commissions and repair costs.
2. **MLS listing:** PR lists through an agent, often at retail price. Competition is higher.
3. **Court auction:** Some jurisdictions require auction for estate properties. Competitive but transparent pricing.

Investors who reach personal representatives early — before an agent is engaged — have the best opportunity to negotiate directly and close faster than MLS competition.

## Key Parties to Understand

**Personal Representative (PR):** The legally authorized decision-maker. This is your contact. All offers must go through the PR, not other heirs.

**Heirs/beneficiaries:** They inherit the proceeds but do not control the sale. Multiple heirs can slow the process significantly — another reason to prioritize low heir-count leads.

**Probate attorney:** Most PRs hire an attorney to navigate the process. Building relationships with probate attorneys can generate referrals.

**Probate referee/assessor:** Some states appoint an independent appraiser to value estate assets. This appraised value often sets a floor for acceptable offers.

## State Variations

Probate law varies significantly by state. Key variations include:

- **Independent vs. supervised administration:** Independent allows the PR to act without court approval at each step.
- **Small estate thresholds:** Many states have simplified procedures for small estates (typically under $150,000-$200,000 in gross value), which may bypass full probate.
- **Affidavit transfers:** Some assets transfer via affidavit without probate entirely.
- **Homestead exemptions:** Some states protect a surviving spouse's right to remain in the home, complicating sale timelines.

Always verify your target state's probate code or consult a local probate attorney before scaling outreach in a new market.
