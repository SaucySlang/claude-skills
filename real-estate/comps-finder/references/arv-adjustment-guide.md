# ARV Adjustment Guide

Adjustments normalize comparable sales to the subject property, making each comp equivalent on paper before averaging. The principle: you are asking "what would this comp have sold for if it were identical to the subject property?" Every significant feature difference between a comp and the subject requires an adjustment.

## Standard Adjustment Amounts

These figures reflect typical residential markets. Regional variation of ±20% is normal. Always verify against local appraiser standards when preparing lender packages.

| Feature | Adjustment | Direction |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Square footage | $10 per sqft difference | + if subject > comp, - if subject < comp |
| Bedroom | $5,000 per bedroom | + if subject has more, - if comp has more |
| Full bathroom | $3,000 per bathroom | + if subject has more, - if comp has more |
| Half bathroom | $1,500 per bathroom | + if subject has more, - if comp has more |
| Garage stall | $8,000 per stall | + if subject has more, - if comp has more |
| Pool (inground) | $15,000 flat | + if subject has pool and comp does not; - if comp has pool |
| Condition tier | $7,500 per tier step | See condition tier table below |

**Condition Tiers (lowest to highest):**
- Distressed (0) — major deferred maintenance, not habitable as-is
- Fair (1) — functional but dated, some systems need replacement
- Average (2) — normal wear, meets market expectations minimally
- Good (3) — well-maintained, minor updates
- Updated (4) — renovated kitchen/baths, modern finishes
- Excellent (5) — like new, high-end finishes throughout

A comp that is "updated" selling to a "distressed" subject gets a negative condition adjustment of 4 tiers × $7,500 = -$30,000.

## When to Adjust vs. Discard

**Adjust when:** The feature difference is standard and quantifiable (sqft, beds, baths, garage, pool). Adjustments under $15,000 total are routine.

**Consider discarding when:**
- Total adjustments exceed $20,000-$25,000 (the comp is too different to be reliable)
- The feature difference is location-based (view, busy road, flood zone, proximity to commercial) — these are very difficult to adjust reliably
- The comp has a feature entirely absent from the subject's market (e.g., guesthouse, detached garage with apartment) that would require speculative adjustment

## How Appraisers Think About Adjustments

Licensed appraisers use paired sales analysis to derive market-supported adjustment amounts. They find two nearly identical sales that differ in only one feature, then measure the price difference attributable to that feature. This produces market-derived adjustments versus rule-of-thumb figures.

For investment underwriting purposes, the standard figures above are sufficient. However, if your ARV is being used to support a hard money or bank loan, the lender will order an independent appraisal. If your adjustments deviate significantly from what the appraiser finds, your deal may be recalculated.

## Weighting Logic

Not all comps deserve equal influence over the final ARV. Assign higher weight to comps that are:
- More recent (sold in the last 90 days vs. 6-12 months ago)
- Closer in distance (within 0.25 miles vs. 0.5+ miles)
- Arm's-length transactions
- Requiring fewer total adjustments

A common approach: rank each comp 1 (weakest) to 5 (strongest) and divide each rank by the sum of all ranks. This produces weights that sum to 1.0 and give the best comps the most influence on the final ARV.

## Common Mistakes

- Adjusting for location differences using flat dollar amounts — location value is market-specific and should be estimated with extreme care or avoided
- Using the same comp in both the ARV and the deal analysis without disclosing it — comps should be independently verified
- Making large adjustments without disclosing them — any adjustment over $10,000 should be noted in the comp report with a brief rationale
- Failing to adjust for condition — condition is the most commonly under-adjusted factor in investment underwriting
