# Assignment Fee Strategy

## How to Price Assignment Fees

Your assignment fee is not arbitrary — it is the residual value left in a deal after the end buyer achieves their required return. Price it correctly and deals flow. Price it too high and you lose buyers. Price it too low and you leave money on the table.

The formula:
```
Assignment Fee = Buyer's MAO - Your Purchase Price - Buffer
```

Where:
- **Buyer's MAO** = ARV × 0.70 (flipper) or ARV × 0.75 (landlord) - Repair Cost
- **Buffer** = 3-5% of purchase price to protect the buyer's margin
- **Your Purchase Price** = what you contracted with the seller

The buffer matters. Buyers experience cost overruns, holding period extensions, and market softness. Build a cushion so your deal still pencils when things go slightly sideways for your buyer.

## Market-Rate Benchmarks by Deal Type

**Fix-and-flip deals:**
- Typical assignment fee range: $8,000 - $35,000
- Fee as % of ARV: 3-10% is normal
- Buyers expect: 20-25% gross profit margin after all costs

**Landlord/buy-and-hold deals:**
- Typical assignment fee range: $5,000 - $20,000
- Fee as % of ARV: 2-7%
- Buyers expect: 8-12% gross cap rate on purchase price + repairs

**Multi-family and developer deals:**
- Fees can be $50,000+ for the right deal
- Pricing is more negotiable — developers model returns differently
- Relationship matters more than formula

**Small deals (under $100k ARV):**
- Minimum fee floor: $5,000
- Never accept less — below $5k, the deal math rarely justifies your time

## Negotiating With Buyers

Most buyers will push back on your fee. This is normal. Your response:

**Anchor on buyer value:** "Your all-in number is $[X]. At ARV of $[Y], you're looking at $[Z] gross profit — that's a [%] margin. This deal works for you."

**Resist the first counter:** If a buyer immediately asks you to reduce the fee, hold. Their first counter is rarely their real limit. Say: "I hear you — what number would make this a definite yes for you?"

**Know your floor before the conversation:** Your minimum fee covers costs plus your baseline ($5,000). Below that, walk away and remarket to another buyer.

**Compete on certainty, not price:** "My contract is solid, the seller is motivated, and the title is clear. You're not paying for just the deal — you're paying for certainty and speed."

## When to Double-Close Instead of Assign

Double-close when your fee exceeds approximately $30,000, or when the buyer is likely to object to your profit margin being visible on the HUD. In a double-close, two separate transactions are recorded. Your fee is embedded in the spread between Transaction A (you buy from seller) and Transaction B (buyer buys from you). The seller sees only their proceeds. The buyer sees only their purchase price.

Transactional funding cost: typically 1-3 days of hard money at 1-2% flat fee. Budget this into your numbers before choosing a double-close over assignment.

## Protecting Your Fee

Your biggest risk: the buyer and seller connect directly and cut you out of the deal.

How to protect yourself:
- Always have a signed assignment agreement before sharing the seller's contact info
- Collect a non-refundable deposit before the buyer communicates with the title company
- Include a non-circumvention clause in your assignment agreement
- Work with title companies that understand wholesale transactions and will not inadvertently connect parties
- Do not market deals on public platforms (Facebook groups, Craigslist) before the assignment is signed

The assignment deposit is your insurance. A buyer with $2,000-$5,000 in non-refundable money on the table will not walk away casually. Always require it.
