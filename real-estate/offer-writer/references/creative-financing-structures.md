# Creative Financing Structures

A reference guide to the most common creative financing strategies used in off-market real estate investing — how each works, legal considerations, and when to apply them.

---

## Subject-To Existing Financing

**What it is:** The buyer purchases the property and takes title while the seller's existing mortgage remains in place. The buyer makes the mortgage payments going forward but the loan stays in the seller's name.

**How it works:**
1. Seller deeds the property to buyer via a warranty or quitclaim deed
2. Existing mortgage is not paid off at closing
3. Buyer services the loan (makes monthly payments to lender)
4. Title is in buyer's name; loan obligation remains with seller

**Benefits:**
- Preserves the seller's low interest rate (valuable in high-rate environments)
- Minimal cash needed at closing
- Fast to execute — no new financing required

**Risks:**
- Due-on-sale clause: Most mortgages include a clause allowing the lender to call the full balance due if the property is transferred. In practice, lenders rarely accelerate performing loans, but the legal risk exists.
- Seller credit risk: The mortgage appears on the seller's credit report until paid off. If buyer defaults, the seller's credit suffers.
- Disclosure required: Always disclose the due-on-sale risk to the seller in writing before execution.

**Best use:** Pre-foreclosure, tired landlords with low-rate mortgages, sellers with little equity.

## Seller Carryback Financing (Seller Finance)

**What it is:** The seller acts as the bank. The buyer pays the seller directly over time via a promissory note secured by a deed of trust (or mortgage) recorded against the property.

**How it works:**
1. Buyer pays a down payment at close
2. Seller carries the remaining balance as a private note
3. Buyer makes monthly principal and interest payments to seller
4. Property is fully transferred to buyer at closing
5. Note is recorded against the property — seller has security interest

**Benefits:**
- Higher purchase price for seller (compensates for deferring lump sum)
- Seller receives regular income stream
- Potential tax advantage: installment sale treatment spreads capital gains over multiple years (consult a CPA)
- Simpler underwriting than traditional lenders

**Documentation required:** Promissory note, deed of trust (or mortgage), loan servicing agreement, disclosure of all terms.

**Best use:** Free-and-clear properties, retirement income seekers, sellers who cannot accept cash due to tax considerations.

## Wraparound Mortgage

**What it is:** A form of seller financing where the seller's existing mortgage remains in place and the buyer's new seller-finance note "wraps around" it. The seller collects a higher payment from the buyer than they owe on the underlying mortgage, keeping the spread.

**How it works:**
- Buyer's note: $200,000 at 7% = $1,330/month to seller
- Seller's underlying mortgage: $120,000 at 4% = $573/month to lender
- Seller's monthly spread: $757/month

**Legal note:** Wraparounds carry the same due-on-sale risk as subject-to. Most commonly used in states where deeds of trust allow it.

## Option Agreement (Lease-Option / Purchase Option)

**What it is:** The buyer secures the right to purchase a property at a set price within a defined window, without being obligated to buy. Often combined with a lease.

**Components:**
- Option fee (non-refundable): typically 1–5% of purchase price
- Strike price: the agreed purchase price, fixed for the option period
- Option term: typically 1–3 years
- Lease terms (if combined): monthly rent, maintenance responsibilities

**Best use:** Properties needing entitlement work, buyers assembling capital, sellers who are not yet ready to sell but are open to locking in a price.

---

## Land Contract (Contract for Deed)

**What it is:** Buyer makes payments to seller directly; seller retains legal title until the contract is paid in full or a balloon payment is made. Buyer holds equitable title during the contract period.

**Key difference from seller finance:** In seller finance, buyer gets title at closing. In a land contract, seller retains legal title until payoff.

**Risk to buyer:** If buyer defaults, seller can forfeit the contract and reclaim the property without foreclosure in some states — a faster process that benefits sellers but leaves buyers vulnerable.

**States with favorable land contract law:** Indiana, Michigan, Ohio, Wisconsin. Rules vary significantly by state.

## Choosing the Right Structure

| Scenario | Recommended Structure |
|---|---|
| Seller has low-rate mortgage | Subject-to |
| Seller owns free and clear, wants income | Seller carryback |
| Seller wants spread on existing mortgage | Wraparound |
| Buyer needs time to arrange financing | Purchase option |
| Long-term installment with title delay | Land contract |
| Seller needs cash now, partial carry | Hybrid (cash + short carry) |
